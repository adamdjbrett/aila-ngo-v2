## Who Matilda Joslyn Gage was

Matilda Joslyn Gage (1826–1898) was raised in a Cicero, New York home that served as a station on the Underground Railroad. She was an abolitionist before she was a suffragist. She led the National Woman Suffrage Association alongside Susan B. Anthony and Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and she co-authored the first three volumes of the *History of Woman Suffrage*.

She also went where her colleagues would not. In *Woman, Church and State* (1893) she named Christian domination as a root of women's oppression. She founded the Woman's National Liberal Union to defend the separation of church and state. She looked to the Haudenosaunee, where women held real political authority, and saw proof that equality was possible. Of our governance she wrote, "Never was justice more perfect, never was civilization higher." She publicly opposed the federal government's treatment of Native nations. In 1893 the Wolf Clan of the Mohawk Nation adopted her and named her Karonienhawi, "She Who Holds the Sky."

For all of this, she was written out of the history she helped make. The historian Margaret Rossiter later coined the term "the Matilda Effect" for the way women's achievements are erased or credited to men, and she took the name from Gage.

## Who Sally was

Sally Roesch Wagner (1942–2025) spent her life reversing that erasure. She held one of the first doctorates in women's studies awarded in this country. She bought and restored Gage's Fayetteville home and opened it as a center for dialogue. In *Sisters in Spirit* and *We Want Equal Rights*, she insisted on a truth many did not want to hear: the early suffragists learned what freedom looked like from Haudenosaunee women. Sally listened to us, credited us, and stood with us. We miss her.

## Why now

We gather at a time when the rights Gage and Sally fought for are under open attack.

**Bodily autonomy and the right to choose.** Since Dobbs, 14 states have imposed total or six-week abortion bans. A Johns Hopkins study published this April links those bans to a rise in pregnancy-associated deaths. Five states allow no exception for a woman's health. In August a federal judge struck down Idaho's ban on that ground and wrote, "A pregnant woman's health is not a state resource to be allocated at the legislature's whim." Idaho has appealed.

**Child marriage.** It remains legal in 33 states. Between 2000 and 2021, nearly 315,000 children were married in the United States. About 86 percent of them were girls wed to adult men.

**Sexual harassment and assault.** The CDC's newest national survey finds that 45 percent of women have experienced contact sexual violence and nearly a third have been sexually harassed at work. One in ten women faced technology-facilitated sexual violence in a single year.

**Access to care.** Nearly half of U.S. hospitals now lack a maternity ward, and more than 700 have closed theirs since 2010. Medicaid pays for 41 percent of births in this country, and it faces deep cuts beginning in January.

Gage would recognize every one of these fights. Sally would tell us to get to work.

All proceeds support the Foundation's educational programs, outreach events, and social justice work in the community.
